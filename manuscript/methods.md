# Methods

## Data source and feature representation

Reference protein sequences for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes were obtained from the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD). Each sequence was represented as a numeric feature vector combining Pfeature-derived compositional descriptors (amino acid composition, dipeptide and tripeptide composition, physicochemical property composition, repetitive-residue and repetitive-property information, Shannon entropy, conjoint triad descriptors, and quasi-sequence-order/pseudo-amino-acid-composition terms) with ProFET-derived sequence-level summary statistics, for a combined 6,206 sequences and 1,040 raw features. This composition-based representation follows an established line of work in which alignment-free, fixed-length descriptors of sequence composition — rather than raw sequence or alignment-based similarity — are used as classifier input for resistance-related prediction tasks, an approach shown to generalize well across pathogens and resistance mechanisms while remaining computationally tractable at scale [1,2].

Gene families represented by fewer than ten sequences were excluded, since one-vs-rest classification and cross-validated evaluation are unreliable below this threshold; this restricted the working dataset to 5,479 sequences across 84 gene families. Feature columns were cleaned prior to modeling: a systematic upstream extraction gap affecting the repetitive-property-information (PRI) columns for a single family (PDC beta-lactamase) was explicitly re-nulled rather than left as silently imputed values, all remaining missing values (15,351 cells) were imputed with the per-column median, and five zero-variance columns were dropped, yielding a final feature matrix of 5,479 sequences by 1,035 features.

## Model architecture

Two complementary classification architectures were trained on the same feature matrix. The primary architecture followed a one-vs-rest design: 84 independent binary LightGBM classifiers, each distinguishing one gene family from all others, trained with 300 estimators, a maximum of 15 leaves, a learning rate of 0.05, and class reweighting (`is_unbalance=True`) to compensate for the rarity of any single family relative to the full sequence set. Gradient-boosted tree ensembles of this kind have been repeatedly favored over both classical statistical scoring methods and deep architectures for AMR-related genomic classification tasks, on the basis of competitive accuracy combined with native support for feature-importance attribution [3,4].

A secondary, single-model architecture was trained for direct classification of a sequence into one of the 84 families without querying 84 separate models: a LightGBM classifier with a multiclass (softmax) objective spanning all families simultaneously. Because LightGBM's multiclass objective fits one tree per class per boosting round, the estimator count was reduced to 100 (from 300 in the binary case) to keep training time tractable — at 84 classes, 300 rounds corresponds to roughly 25,200 individual trees rather than 300 — with 31 leaves and a learning rate of 0.1. Class imbalance, which the binary models address via `is_unbalance`, has no direct multiclass equivalent in LightGBM and was instead handled by weighting each training sequence inversely to its family's size (`class_weight="balanced"`), consistent with standard practice for imbalanced multiclass genomic classification [3].

## Cross-validation design

Model evaluation used five-fold cross-validation (reduced proportionally for families with fewer than five positive examples), computed via stratified, grouped k-fold splitting rather than a plain random or single held-out split. Standard random cross-validation risks optimistic bias when near-identical sequences are split across training and evaluation folds, since a classifier can then appear to generalize by effectively memorizing a near-duplicate of a training example rather than learning generalizable sequence-composition signal — a concern most rigorously addressed in AMR machine learning by clustering sequences at a fixed identity threshold (e.g., with CD-HIT) before assigning cross-validation folds [5]. True sequence-identity clustering was not available for this dataset; as an approximate substitute, near-duplicate groups were instead identified directly in standardized feature space, connecting any two sequences within a Euclidean distance of 1.0 (chosen by inspecting the empirical nearest-neighbor distance distribution, which showed a clear separation between a tight near-identical cluster and the broader within-family distribution) via a radius-neighbors graph, and constraining every sequence within a connected component to the same fold. This substitution is an approximation of true sequence-identity clustering, not a formal equivalent, since coincidentally similar composition does not guarantee true sequence similarity; a post hoc check confirmed no single near-duplicate group exceeded 50% of any one family's sequences, indicating the grouping did not itself distort stratification. Stratified cross-validation of this kind — explicitly preserving class proportions across folds to mitigate the effect of class imbalance on fold-level evaluation — mirrors the design used in other recent interpretable AMR classification work [6].

## Feature importance

Feature importance was assessed with two complementary methods rather than a single measure, motivated by the well-documented limitation that impurity- or gain-based importance (the estimator's own free byproduct) is biased and does not measure a feature's true contribution to held-out predictive performance. A two-stage design was used to keep permutation importance computationally tractable: gain-based importance from the fitted booster first shortlisted the top 80 candidate features per fold, after which permutation importance (five reshuffles per feature) was computed only on that shortlist, since exhaustive permutation importance across all 1,035 features was measured directly at approximately nine hours for the full set of 84 families and was not viable. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, computed on the complete feature set via the tree-specific explainer, were treated as the primary importance signal rather than a secondary check: for families whose held-out average precision approached the theoretical ceiling of 1.0, permutation importance collapsed toward zero even for features known to matter, because the model had learned multiple redundant, individually sufficient features and shuffling any single one caused no measurable performance loss. This divergence between the two importance measures near the top of the performance range is consistent with reports that SHAP retains discriminative resolution among instance-level or near-saturated feature contributions where perturbation-based importance measures do not [7,8]. Consistent with this rationale, recent interpretable AMR prediction pipelines have similarly adopted SHAP as the primary attribution method precisely for its resolution advantage over permutation- and impurity-based alternatives [6,9,10].

## Evaluation metrics and final model deployment

For both architectures, discrimination was summarized using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and average precision (AP), computed from out-of-fold predicted probabilities and therefore threshold-independent. Accuracy, F1, and confusion-matrix counts were additionally computed at a fixed probability threshold of 0.5; accuracy was interpreted only alongside F1, since with class ratios as skewed as those in several rare families (as few as ten positive sequences among 5,479 total), a threshold-based accuracy figure can appear favorable while reflecting little more than the base rate of the majority class. For the multiclass architecture, cross-validated performance was additionally summarized as top-1 accuracy and both macro- and weighted-averaged F1 across all 84 classes simultaneously, with per-family recall reported to allow direct comparison against the corresponding one-vs-rest family's held-out performance.

Following cross-validation, one final model per architecture was trained on the complete dataset (all 5,479 sequences, no further held-out split) for deployment — for the one-vs-rest architecture, one such model per family (84 total); for the multiclass architecture, a single model spanning all families. These final models were saved in LightGBM's native serialized format rather than a language- or library-version-specific serialization such as Python's `pickle`, to avoid tying the saved models to a specific machine's software environment. Serialization correctness was confirmed for every saved model by reloading it and verifying that predictions reproduced the pre-serialization output exactly; this check validates that saving and reloading is lossless, and is explicitly distinct from a generalization estimate, since a model's performance on the same data it was trained on is not an unbiased measure of accuracy. The cross-validated metrics described above, computed exclusively on held-out folds, remain the estimate of how these final models are expected to perform on genuinely new sequences.
